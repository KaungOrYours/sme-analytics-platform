**Headline finding, flag prominently**: including `irish` makes the automated pipeline look better than the
manual baseline on the development group (+0.0172); excluding it *flips the sign* (−0.0240) and the
loss count (18/27 losses either way, but now a clear majority of a smaller, cleaner set). The entire
development-group RQ1 headline direction depends on whether one leakage-saturated dataset is in the sample.
This is a substantive methodological point for the discussion chapter, not just a footnote: an unweighted,
small-N (4-dataset) pooled average is fragile to exactly the kind of per-dataset artifact described below —
arguably a stronger argument for the dissertation's reframed "study the warnings, not the leaderboard" angle
than a clean win for either pipeline would have been.

Final-evaluation group shows a real, cleaner automated-pipeline edge (automated wins or ties on 26/27 runs)
with no comparable leakage artifact in any of `labor`/`mux6`/`bupa` found so far — worth double-checking the
other two datasets the same way `irish` was checked, before leaning on this as a clean result.

## Case study: `irish`'s `Educational_level` leakage (see `docs/datasets.md` for full writeup)

`Educational_level` perfectly determines `target` in `irish` (zero exceptions, 500/500 rows), discovered
because the automated pipeline hit AUROC=1.0 on every seed at the clean condition — triggering the project's
own "treat 99-100% accuracy as a bug until proven otherwise" rule. Verified as a dataset-level artifact, not a
pipeline bug. Random Forest (the automated pipeline's selected model) exploits the leak fully via threshold
splits; logistic regression (the manual baseline) only partially exploits it because the leak is non-monotonic
in the raw integer coding PMLB uses, capping LR at a believable 0.82–0.90.

**This survives corruption**: `irish`'s automated-pipeline S_test stays at exactly 1.0 even at 35%
missing-value corruption on the training partition (see `evaluation/results/development_rq2_scores.json`) —
the leak is robust enough that imputation doesn't meaningfully disturb it. Worth a sentence on this
robustness specifically, since it's a slightly stronger and more surprising claim than "leakage exists."

Also worth noting directly: at the clean condition, `sigma_cv=0` for all 3 `irish` seeds, so the `sigma_cv >
0.10` diagnostic *cannot* fire there by construction — a saturated run has zero CV variance and is therefore
invisible to a variance-based diagnostic, even though it is arguably the single least trustworthy result in
the whole development group. The app's own excluded `suspected_leakage` warning (ceiling 0.98) would have
caught this; the formally-scored diagnostics in this evaluation would not have, on their own. That contrast
is worth a paragraph in the discussion chapter — it's a real limitation of the chosen diagnostic set, surfaced
by an actual run rather than argued in the abstract.

## RQ3's combination rule (2026-10-20) — closing a gap in the committed design, not a new experiment

`evaluation_protocol.md`'s RQ3 wording was never fully scored until now: *"Do the diagnostics **plus a
combination rule**, fit on the development group, show similar discrimination on the disjoint
final-evaluation group?"* Earlier passes scored `n/p` and `sigma_cv` individually but never built the
combination rule that sentence actually asks for. No new experiment runs were needed — this reuses
Experiment 1's existing results. Built as `evaluation/score_rq3_combination.py`.

**Rule candidates**: logical OR (triggered if either diagnostic fires) and logical AND (triggered only if
both fire) — no trained meta-learner, deliberately, since that would blur the exact distinction
`docs/literature_matrix.md` draws against Lahoti, Gummadi & Weikum (2023)'s trained Risk Advisor. **Procedure**:
score both rules on the development group only, lock whichever has the higher MCC there, then apply that
one locked rule — untouched — to the final-evaluation group and report its transfer.

| Step | Rule | MCC | Precision | Recall | False-alarm rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fit (development) | OR | **0.153** [CI −0.18, 0.44] | 0.133 | 0.667 | 0.394 |
| Fit (development) | AND | −0.051 [CI −0.12, 0.00] | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.03 |
| **Locked rule = OR** → tested on final-evaluation | OR | **0.367** [CI 0.00, 0.59] | 0.231 | **1.0** | 0.417 |

**The honest finding**: the locked OR rule transfers well — it even improves on the disjoint
final-evaluation group (MCC 0.153 → 0.367, catching every single poor run, recall=1.0). That's a genuine
positive RQ3 answer: the combination rule is not a fluke of the development group, it generalises.

**But it does not beat `n/p` alone.** `n/p<10` by itself on the final-evaluation group scores MCC=0.50 (see
the RQ2/RQ3 table earlier in this document) — *better* than the OR-combined rule's 0.367. The reason is
visible directly in the confusion matrices: both achieve the same recall (1.0, 3/3 poor runs caught), but
the combined rule has 10 false alarms where `n/p` alone has only 6 — OR-ing in `sigma_cv` adds four extra
false triggers and catches zero additional true positives. This is exactly what the rest of today's findings
predict: `sigma_cv` has shown no positive discriminative value anywhere in this project, so logically-OR-ing
it into a combination rule can only ever add noise, never signal. **This directly validates the frozen
protocol's own stated caution (Section 1): "a combined signal is not assumed superior to either alone" — and
here, empirically, it measurably is not.** Good news for the write-up: this isn't a null result, it's a
mechanistically explained one that ties every strand of today's evaluation together in one place.

## Why does `n/p` fail under duplicate corruption? A re-analysis, no new runs (2026-10-20)

Motivating question: `n/p<10` goes from a defensible MCC=0.265 on development-duplicates to a confidently
negative MCC=−0.322 on final-evaluation-duplicates (see the Experiments 2-3 table above). Original
hypothesis: duplicate rows inflate `n` without adding real information, so maybe `n/p` is being fooled by
fake sample-size growth crossing the `<10` boundary. Tested directly against data already collected (no new
experiment runs) by reconstructing each run's "honest" `n` — the pre-duplication training-set size, looked
up from Experiment 1's own clean-condition result for the same dataset+seed — and comparing whether the
`n/p<10` trigger would have fired differently using honest `n` instead of the duplication-inflated `n`.

**The hypothesis is false**: across all 42 duplicate-condition runs (both groups), the trigger outcome is
identical whether honest or inflated `n` is used — zero disagreements. Duplication at 15-35% simply isn't
large enough to push any of these 7 datasets' `n/p` across the `<10` line either way. Worth stating plainly:
a plausible-sounding mechanism, tested and ruled out, not assumed away.

**The real mechanism, found by then asking which specific runs `n/p` misses**: every single false negative
under duplicate corruption — `appendicitis` (n/p 14-16), `biomed` (n/p 38-45), `bupa` (n/p 63-75), 10 of 10
across both groups — occurs in a dataset where `n/p` is already comfortably *above* 10, the threshold meant
to signal "safe." Conversely, the one dataset with triggers that sometimes land on real poor runs
(`molecular_biology_promoters`, n/p 1.7-2.0) triggers on every single run regardless of outcome, because its
baseline `n/p` is so low the diagnostic has no room to discriminate at all there. `bupa` is the starkest case:
**100% of its 6 duplicate-condition runs are poor** (G up to +0.26), all with `n/p` in the 63-75 range — a
dataset that looks maximally "safe" by the sample-size-adequacy logic `n/p` encodes, failing every single
time under a corruption type that has nothing to do with sample-size adequacy.

**This is a construct-validity finding, not a calibration one.** `n/p` measures whether a dataset has enough
rows relative to features — a reasonable proxy for *estimation* instability. Duplicate-induced CV-fold
leakage (Finding #2 under Experiments 2-3 above: a duplicated row landing in both a training fold and its
own validation fold) is a *data-integrity* failure, not a sample-size one — more rows, even fake duplicated
ones, cannot protect against it, and may make it easier (more opportunities for a duplicate pair to straddle
a fold boundary). The diagnostic isn't miscalibrated for this failure mode; it's answering a question that
isn't the one duplicate corruption asks. This is a sharper, more defensible explanation than "the threshold
needs tuning," and it's the kind of finding that only shows up by testing a specific, falsifiable hypothesis
and following the data when it says no — worth presenting in the discussion chapter exactly in that order
(hypothesis stated, tested, rejected, replaced), not just as the conclusion on its own.

## Experiments 2-3 — duplicates and outliers (extension beyond the committed design, 2026-10-20)

**Status**: run the same day as the aggregate-CI revision above, at the student's explicit request to go
beyond the PDM minimum and use spare compute budget ("you can run for 2 hours if you need"). These are *not*
part of `evaluation_protocol.md`'s committed, frozen design — that document commits to missing-values only
(Section 7) and explicitly allows a conditional expansion "only if time and results allow." This is that
expansion, clearly labelled as such, built in a separate script (`evaluation/full_run_extended.py`) that
leaves `full_run.py` (Experiment 1) untouched. `inconsistent_categories` (the 4th available injector) was
checked and excluded: all 7 PMLBmini/TabMini datasets are 100% numerically encoded with zero object-dtype
columns, so that injector (which only perturbs object-dtype columns) would be a complete no-op on this
dataset source — worth a sentence in the methodology chapter on why that error type isn't tested here, not
silently skipped.

Same grid shape as Experiment 1 otherwise: {15%, 35%} corruption rate × 3 seeds × {automated, manual}, per
dataset per group. (0% isn't re-run — it's already the Experiment 1 clean condition.) RQ2 diagnostic scoring
scope, thresholds, delta_G, and the aggregate/per-run bootstrap methods are all identical to Experiment 1 and
the revision above — nothing about the *scoring* changed, only the corruption applied to the training data.

### RQ1 — automated vs. manual, by corruption type (S_test mean diff, automated − manual)

| Corruption family | Development (all 4) | Development (excl. `irish`) | Final-evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Missing values (Experiment 1) | +0.0172 | −0.0240 | +0.0694 |
| Duplicates (Experiment 2) | +0.0148 | −0.0284 | +0.0607 |
| Outliers (Experiment 3) | **+0.1338** | **+0.1067** | **+0.1687** |

**Strongest, most mechanistically well-grounded RQ1 finding in the whole project so far**: under outlier
corruption, the automated pipeline beats the manual baseline clearly and robustly, with or without `irish` in
the sample, and the win margin is 6-12x larger than under missing-values or duplicate corruption. This has a
clean, citable explanation, not a mystery: Random Forest (the automated pipeline's usual pick) is well known
to be far more robust to outliers than logistic regression fit on `StandardScaler`-scaled features (an
8-sigma outlier distorts the scaler's mean/variance estimate and therefore every coefficient, not just the
corrupted cells) — this is textbook, not a project-specific artifact, and should be cited as such rather than
presented as a novel observation. Missing-values and duplicates both show the same `irish`-sensitive
fragility documented for Experiment 1 (positive with it in, negative without it) — the pattern is not
specific to one corruption type, which is itself worth stating: it's a property of including a
leakage-saturated dataset in a 4-dataset pooled average, not of the missing-values protocol specifically.

### RQ2/RQ3 — diagnostic scoring, by corruption type (automated-pipeline runs, MCC [95% bootstrap CI])

| Corruption | Group | n_poor/n_runs | n/p MCC [CI] | sigma_cv MCC [CI] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Missing values | Development | 3/36 (8.3%) | 0.29 [−0.11, 0.61] | −0.148 [−0.25, 0.00] |
| Missing values | Final-eval | 3/27 (11.1%) | 0.50 [0.00, 0.77] | −0.147 [−0.26, 0.00] |
| Duplicates | Development | 7/24 (29.2%) | 0.265 [−0.16, 0.70] | −0.193 [−0.36, 0.00] |
| Duplicates | Final-eval | 7/18 (38.9%) | **−0.322 [−0.67, 0.12]** | 0.0 (degenerate — never triggers, 0/18) |
| Outliers | Development | **0/24 (0.0%)** | 0.0 (degenerate — no poor cases at all) | 0.0 (degenerate — no poor cases at all) |
| Outliers | Final-eval | 2/18 (11.1%) | 0.125 [−0.29, 0.57] | −0.219 [−0.40, 0.00] |

**Three findings here, all real, all worth separate treatment in the discussion chapter:**

1. **Duplicates produce far more "poor" runs than the other two families** (29-39% vs 8-11%), and the reason
   is mechanistic, not noise: duplicate rows are injected into the training partition *before* the CV split
   inside `run_automated`/`run_manual_baseline`, so a duplicated row can land in a training fold and its
   near-identical twin in that fold's validation split within the same CV run — genuine fold-level leakage,
   which inflates `S_CV` relative to the untouched test set and is exactly the kind of validation-to-test
   optimism `G` is designed to catch. This is good news for the diagnostics' construct validity (the signed-G
   definition correctly flags a real leakage mechanism when one is actually present) and a clean, defensible
   point for the methodology chapter: *the corruption family that most resembles a genuine leakage pathway
   produces the richest "poor" signal, exactly as the theory predicts.*
2. **Outlier corruption almost never triggers "poor" at all** (0% in development, 11% in final-eval) — because
   outliers, in this training setup, predominantly push `G` *negative* (the CV estimate becomes pessimistic
   relative to test, not optimistic — an outlier-corrupted fold's CV score degrades more than the clean test
   score does). Since the frozen "poor" definition (`G > delta_G`) is deliberately one-sided — it targets
   optimism specifically, not instability in either direction, per `evaluation_protocol.md` Section 4's own
   stated reasoning — it has essentially no sensitivity to this particular corruption family's typical failure
   signature. This is not a bug in the definition; it is a scope limitation that should be stated plainly: the
   formal RQ2/RQ3 evaluation, as frozen, is well-suited to detecting leakage-like optimism (missing-values,
   duplicates) and poorly suited to detecting outlier-induced instability, even though outliers clearly do
   disrupt the manual baseline badly (see the RQ1 table above).
3. **`n/p<10`'s discrimination does not transfer cleanly across corruption types**: positive on missing-values
   (both groups) and duplicates-development, but reverses to a confidently negative MCC on
   duplicates-final-evaluation (CI [−0.67, 0.12], point estimate −0.322). This complicates the clean
   "preliminary transfer" story from the aggregate-CI revision above — `n/p`'s apparent transfer success was
   specific to the missing-values family, not a general property of the diagnostic. State this as the honest
   finding it is: *n/p's validity appears to depend on which error type is present, which is itself informative
   about the limits of a single, static threshold-based diagnostic — and arguably strengthens the overall
   thesis (the dissertation's reframed novelty claim) rather than weakening it, since "under what conditions do
   built-in diagnostics fail to transfer" is a more interesting question than "does this one diagnostic work."*
   `sigma_cv>0.10` remains the more consistent finding throughout: its MCC point estimate is never positive in
   any non-degenerate cell across all three corruption families and both groups — the strongest, most
   triangulated claim in the whole evaluation so far.

## Experiment 4 — sample-size sensitivity (the other Section 7 conditional expansion, 2026-10-20)

**Design**: the other half of the Section 7 "conditional expansion if time allows" clause (Experiments 2-3
exercised the corruption-family half). Clean condition only, no corruption combined in, to keep this a
single-factor test. For each dataset/seed, the outer test set is kept at full size (never subsampled) and
only the training partition is halved (stratified, seeded) — isolating "less data to train on" from "less
data to evaluate on." Safety-checked first (minority class stays >= 8 after halving for every one of the 7
datasets, comfortably above the 5-fold CV floor) and the runner (`evaluation/full_run_sample_size.py`) also
carries a runtime guard that skips and logs rather than crashes if any seed's split ever came in under a
5-example floor. 21 runs total (12 development + 9 final-evaluation), 0 skipped.

| Metric (automated pipeline) | Full-size training | Half-size training |
|---|---|---|
| Mean sigma_cv | 0.048 (0.056 excl. `irish`) | 0.080 (0.093 excl. `irish`) |
| Mean \|G\| | 0.040 | 0.074 |
| Mean S_test | 0.913 | 0.886 |
| Poor runs (G > 0.05) | 1/21 (4.8%) | 7/21 (33.3%) |

**This is the cleanest confirmatory result in the whole project, and it validates the core premise behind the
`n/p` diagnostic directly**, independent of that diagnostic's mixed precision/recall/MCC numbers elsewhere:
halving the training data, with everything else held constant, makes cross-validation noisier (sigma_cv up,
consistent direction in 11/18 non-saturated runs), makes the validation-to-test gap more volatile (mean |G|
nearly doubles), modestly hurts real test performance (S_test down, as expected with less training data), and
— most importantly for RQ2 — **roughly 7x's the poor-outcome rate** (4.8% → 33.3%). In other words: a lower
n (same p, so lower n/p) is directly and strongly associated with more of exactly the failure mode `n/p<10` is
supposed to predict. The diagnostic's underlying *mechanism* is well-supported even where its formal scoring
on the fixed-size dataset manifest was statistically uncertain — worth stating both halves of that honestly:
the mechanism clearly operates in the expected direction, but turning that into a reliable per-dataset
early-warning signal at a FIXED, unknown n/p is a harder, separate question from "does smaller n/p correlate
with worse outcomes in general," and this experiment answers the general correlational question, not the
per-run predictive one.

Not formally scored with precision/recall/MCC (21 runs is too few to do that meaningfully once split by
triggered/not-triggered) — this experiment is reported descriptively, which is itself an honest choice to
state in the methodology chapter rather than force a confusion matrix onto too few data points.
